# 1. Graphs?

Yes, our research centers around graphs. Picture a graph. Perhaps you are imagining a chart or the graph of an equation on a cartesian plane. In the mathematical field of graph theory, graphs have a specific definition, representing a network of nodes connected by edges:

> “A graph $G$ is an ordered pair $(V(G), E(G))$ consisting of a set $V(G)$ of vertices and a set $E(G)$, disjoint from $V(G)$, of edges, together with an incidence function $\psi_G$ that associates with each edge of $G$ an unordered pair of (not necessarily distinct) vertices of $G$.” [1]

We will loosely adopt this set-theoretic definition of graph in our work, because while it may look entirely abstract and different from more visual charts, it too can be applied to model the same relations, and expressed visually.

Pure mathematicians, being precise with their abstractions, distinguish graphs themselves from their set-theoretic or notational, and visual representations. Throughout this text we take a more applied view. Rather than treating abstract objects as more mathematically fundamental than their representations, we will study the representations as mathematical objects in their own right. A function can appear as a curve, an equation, or a table of inputs and outputs; all facets of the same gem.

Graphs were so named for their ability to be represented visually. Perhaps due to this, they are often used in the study of physical networks and relationships. Following our applied view, we ask what if the abstract graph itself was equivalent to the network it represents, perhaps undergoing a single mapping function. The map is not the terrain, but is only one function away.
